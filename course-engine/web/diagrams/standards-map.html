Standards map — who owns what

Click a rung to see what force it actually carries, then compare two classification schemes to see the axis on which they genuinely disagree. Interview one-liner: Tier, Rated and Availability Class are three instruments from three owners measuring three different things — mapping them one-to-one is the field's signature competence failure.

Accreditation is not what most people think. ANSI accredits standards developers and approves documents as American National Standards. It accredits neither documents nor data centres. "ANSI-accredited data centre" is not a thing, and the compression of "ANSI/TIA-942 is accredited by ANSI" into that phrase is the direct source of the error.

2 · Three schemes that are not synonyms

TIA · ANSI-approved SDO

ANSI/TIA-942 Rated-1 – 4

Ratings over facility subsystems, assessed by separately licensed Certification Bodies.

CEN-CENELEC / ISO-IEC

Uptime Tier vs ANSI/TIA-942 Rated

Not synonyms — different evaluation philosophy, different assessor, different decay clock

Axis 1 · evaluation philosophy

Uptime characterises the TIA approach as availability "in terms of a checklist / component count, which does not ensure how the subsystems are tied together to deliver uptime in multiple operation modes (standard, maintenance, post-fault)", and states it has intentionally refrained from developing a design manual or checklist for achieving a Tier level. One scheme evaluates the outcome; the other assesses conformity against prescribed content. A design can pass one and fail the other without either being wrong.

Axis 2 · who assesses, and whose impartiality is checked

TIA owns the standard and licenses separate Certification Bodies, evaluated against ISO/IEC 17020 / 17021 for impartiality, competency and consistency, and does not permit individual auditors to certify. Uptime owns the standard and issues the awards itself, with no external accreditation body named. Both arrangements are coherent. They are not the same kind of assurance and should not be priced as if they were.

Axis 3 · how fast the paper decays

Uptime's Tier Certification of Design Documents expires two years after award. ANSI/TIA-942 Design certification is valid one year, extendable while the facility is non-operational. For constructed facilities the disagreement is sharper: TIA-942 Facilities runs three years with mandatory surveillance audits at years one and two, while no expiry is published for Tier Certification of Constructed Facility. That is not a disagreement about the building — it is a disagreement about whether a constructed-facility attestation is a point-in-time fact or a maintained state.

A buyer who treats "Tier III" and "Rated-3" as interchangeable has silently inherited a different assessor, a different philosophy and roughly a 2× difference in how fast the certificate goes stale.

Uptime Tier vs EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 Availability Class

Not synonyms — one site rating versus a vector of subsystem classes

Axis 1 · one rating vs many

EN 50600 defines availability classes separately for power distribution, environmental control and telecommunications cabling, so "AC4 electrical, AC2 mechanical" is a well-formed statement about a facility. Uptime awards one site rating, governed by the weakest relevant subsystem — its own guidance warns that a concurrently maintainable electrical infrastructure paired with a single-path mechanical system "can only lead to ultimate disappointment" if called Tier III. A bare "Class 4" discards qualifiers that "Tier IV" would not.

Axis 2 · what happens during maintenance

EN 50600 AC4 is described as fault tolerant except during maintenance — it concedes the maintenance state up front and says nothing about physical separation. Tier IV requires fault tolerance and physical compartmentalization of complementary paths. Reading the two as equivalent simultaneously overstates AC4 and understates what Tier IV costs in civil works.

Axis 3 · the shared blind spot — a class is a state, not a plaque

Both schemes label the facility, and the facility changes hourly. Even at Tier IV, "during maintenance activities, the risk of disruption may be elevated." The moment a component is pulled, an N+1 group is running at N and a 2N pair is running at N. A single static label cannot express that — which is precisely why topologies such as 2(N+1) and N+2 exist. Report the class per operating state: normal, each maintenance state, each post-fault state.

Interview move: when someone states a class, ask "of which subsystem, in which operating state, and who assessed it?" All three schemes have an answer. Marketing usually does not.

ANSI/TIA-942 Rated vs EN 50600 / ISO/IEC 22237 Availability Class

Not synonyms — an ordinal versus a vector; they cannot be equated element-wise

Axis 1 · classification geometry

TIA-942 issues Rating levels over four facility subsystems under a licensed Certification Body scheme. EN 50600 splits the same question into three orthogonal dimensions — Availability Classes, Protection Classes and Granularity Levels — with requirements conditional on the operator's own risk analysis. "Rated-3" is a single ordinal; the EN 50600 equivalent is a vector. There is no element-wise mapping between them, and any crosswalk table that offers one is discarding the dimensions it cannot represent.

Axis 2 · what triggers the requirement

A Rated level is a target you design to. An EN 50600 class is conditional on a documented risk analysis, which means two facilities with identical hardware can legitimately carry different classes because their risk analyses differ. The document is not describing the same kind of object.

Axis 3 · level of assembly

Inside the TIA scheme alone, "TIA-942 certified" can mean a Ready certification — that a modular product was designed in accordance with the standard, valid one year with yearly recertification — or a Facilities certification requiring physical onsite inspection, valid three years with surveillance. A modular product's design attestation on a twelve-month clock is not an inspected facility. Uptime has the same split, between Tier-Ready for a manufacturer's product and Tier Certification of Constructed Facility for the site the product lands on.

The four-number availability table that will not die

A retired claim, still in circulation, contradicted by its own purported authority

The claim

Tables mapping Tier I–IV to roughly 99.67 / 99.75 / 99.98 / 99.99 percent site availability (and matching "minutes of downtime per year") appear in slide decks, RFPs, blogs and training material, and were read verbatim in a document uploaded as recently as 2023.

What the authority says

Uptime states that in 2009 it removed all references to expected downtime per year when using the Tier Standard, and that the current Tier Standard of Topology does not assign availability predictions to Tier levels. The number being quoted is attributed to an organisation that says it withdrew it.

Why it matters more than a footnote

A topology classification and an availability prediction are different kinds of claim. Topology is structural and inspectable; predicted availability is a statistical claim requiring a failure model, a population and a measurement period. Quoting the second as though the first delivered it is how a design review ends up with an availability commitment nobody can defend.

If you are asked for a Tier-to-availability number in an interview, the strong answer is: "Uptime withdrew those figures in 2009 — the Tier Standard classifies topology, not predicted availability. If you want an availability number, we have to model it."

"Tier III" as a market adjective — five products, one word

Self-declaration versus awarded conformity — different objects, identical marketing

What the adjective omits

"Tier III" used in marketing names no award, no issuer, no date and no register entry. It gets applied indiscriminately to design-only certifications, to sites deploying pre-validated modular products, and to engagements that produced no award at all.

The document that looks like an award and is not

A Tier Gap Analysis delivers the same review as the first pass of a design-document certification — same reviewers, same first pass, same letterhead — and produces a deficiency report carrying no Tier designation. In an evidence pack, a gap-analysis report and a design certification are near-indistinguishable and mean close to opposite things. Read the award line, not the logo.

Prerequisites differ inside a single scheme

Uptime's Management & Operations criteria identify behaviours "independent of Tiers and the installed infrastructure" and require no Tier award at all; Tier Certification of Operational Sustainability requires both design-document and constructed-facility certification first. Two operations products from one owner, with incompatible prerequisites and different meanings in a data room.

Who owns which hat (disclosed, permitted, and worth knowing)

Roles in this market concentrate. EPI authors DCOS — an operations standard it also certifies against — trains the TIA-942 auditor credentials, has its exams administered by EXIN, and operates as one of TIA's licensed Certification Bodies. Uptime owns the Tier Standard, issues Tier awards, endorses its own accredited-individual credentials, and since acquiring CNet Training in 2023 also sells the training that was previously the market's independent alternative. None of this is improper — the licensing schemes exist to manage exactly this concentration — but any guidance describing these bodies as mutually independent is stale.
